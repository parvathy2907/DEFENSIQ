The proposed framework aims to support ransomware defense decisions by combining dynamic malware analysis, standardized attack–defense mapping, residual-severity estimation, and evolutionary optimization. It connects observed malicious behaviors with candidate defensive mechanisms and evaluates alternative defense combinations to identify promising strategies.

---

## Utility of the Work

The primary utility of this work is to provide a structured and systematic approach to evaluating defensive mechanisms against observed ransomware behaviors. Instead of considering malware detection and defense selection as separate activities, the framework connects behavioral evidence to attack characterization, defense mapping, severity assessment, and optimization.

Cuckoo Sandbox provides the behavioral reports used to identify triggered signatures and observed execution activities. MITRE ATT&CK is used to characterize adversarial techniques, while the project's ATT&CK–D3FEND mapping process identifies candidate defensive mechanisms. Signature impact quantification and LLM-based residual-severity estimation provide severity-related information that the Cooperative Co-Evolutionary Algorithm (CCEA) uses to evaluate candidate defense combinations.

This workflow can help organize attack–defense information, reduce repetitive manual assessment, and support more systematic comparisons of defensive options. Its recommendations are intended to assist decision-making rather than replace established security controls or automatically deploy defensive policies.

### Operational Benefits for Security Teams

#### 1. Automated Behavioral Analysis and TTP Alignment
The framework processes sandbox-generated reports to identify triggered behavioral signatures and relevant execution evidence. These observations are associated with MITRE ATT&CK tactics, techniques, and sub-techniques, providing a standardized way to describe the behaviors observed during analysis.

#### 2. Structured Defense Mapping
The framework uses ATT&CK–D3FEND mappings to associate observed attack techniques with candidate defensive mechanisms. This helps organize the relationship between an attack behavior and the available countermeasures and provides a structured basis for evaluating their relevance.

#### 3. Residual-Severity Assessment
An LLM evaluates an attack–defense pair and estimates the residual severity (RS), representing the estimated severity that remains after considering the defensive mechanism. This assessment can reduce the need for manual evaluation of every candidate pair, although the accuracy of the estimates must be established through experimental validation.

#### 4. Quantitative Defense Prioritization
The impact-quantification and residual-severity components provide numerical inputs for comparing candidate defense combinations. The fitness function allows the optimization process to evaluate alternatives using the project's defined severity measures rather than relying exclusively on informal rankings.

#### 5. Efficient Search of Defense Combinations
When multiple attack signatures have several candidate defenses, the number of possible combinations can become very large. CCEA uses candidate generation, selection, exploration, and mutation to search this space and retain promising solutions. This avoids the need to enumerate every possible combination explicitly, although the result is not guaranteed to be the global optimum.

#### 6. Support for Research and Security Analysis
The framework provides an integrated workflow for examining observed malware behavior, evaluating candidate defensive mechanisms, and comparing the resulting defense strategies. Its structured inputs, severity estimates, optimization outputs, and evaluation metrics can support further research into adaptive ransomware defense.

## Security Problems Addressed

Ransomware defense involves challenges beyond identifying malicious files. Analysts must understand the behavior of an attack, determine which defensive mechanisms are relevant, and assess whether malicious activity may remain after mitigation.

The proposed framework addresses the following security-analysis and decision-making challenges.

### Specific Threat Scenarios

#### 1. Evasive Malware and Incomplete Behavioral Evidence
Ransomware and other malware may use obfuscation, packing, fileless execution, environment checks, or delayed execution to make analysis more difficult. Such techniques can prevent some malicious behaviors from appearing during a particular sandbox execution.

The framework uses observed sandbox behavior as the basis for its analysis and defense mapping. This helps organize the evidence that is available, but it cannot guarantee that every evasive behavior will be observed. Recommendations must therefore be interpreted in the context of the evidence collected.

#### 2. Recovery Inhibition and Data Encryption
Some ransomware attacks attempt to make recovery more difficult by deleting volume shadow copies, disabling recovery mechanisms, or targeting accessible backups. Other behaviors involve encrypting data to disrupt access to files and systems. MITRE ATT&CK describes these behaviors through techniques such as *Inhibit System Recovery* (T1490) and *Data Encrypted for Impact* (T1486).

The framework can represent these observed behaviors, associate them with relevant candidate defenses, and estimate residual severity for the resulting attack–defense pairs. It does not assume that a mapped defense will necessarily prevent the behavior in every environment.

#### 3. Lateral Movement and Expansion of Attack Impact
An attacker may attempt to move from an initially compromised system to additional systems within an organization. If these behaviors are observed and represented in the analysis dataset, they can be evaluated alongside candidate defenses relevant to the corresponding techniques.

Considering multiple observed behaviors helps the framework assess defense combinations in the context of the broader attack scenario rather than evaluating only an isolated malicious action.

#### 4. Difficulty of Comparing Defensive Mechanisms
Different defenses perform different functions. Some restrict operations, some detect suspicious activity, and others isolate processes or strengthen security configurations. Their effectiveness depends on the attack behavior, the control's capabilities, and its configuration.

The framework addresses the analytical challenge of comparing candidate defenses by using structured attack–defense mappings and LLM-based residual-severity estimates. These estimates provide a common input to the optimization process, but their reliability depends on the quality of the supplied evidence and the accuracy of the model.

#### 5. Combinatorial Complexity in Defense Selection
A single malware sample may trigger several signatures, each of which can have multiple candidate defensive mechanisms. Selecting a combination across all these candidates can create a large search space.

CCEA addresses this challenge through an iterative search process that generates candidate combinations, evaluates their fitness, and retains promising solutions. Its goal is to identify an effective defense combination within the configured search process, rather than exhaustively evaluating every possible arrangement.

#### 6. Adapting Defense Selection to Different Attack Behaviors
A defense combination that performs well against one set of observed behaviors may not be the most suitable combination for another. The framework addresses this issue by evaluating candidate defenses against the attack signatures and severity estimates associated with the scenario being considered.

CCEA supports search-based defense selection across these candidate combinations. However, it should not be interpreted as automatically predicting all future attacker adaptations; its recommendations are bounded by the attack dataset, the candidate defense pool, the severity estimates, and the implemented optimization process.

The current optimization approach evaluates candidate combinations using the project's defined severity and fitness measures. Operational factors such as deployment cost, compatibility with legitimate applications, host performance overhead, and false-positive impact are not part of the current fitness function, so the framework does not optimize for them.

The project does not claim improvements in incident-response time or mean time to remediate (MTTR); measuring those would need a dedicated study.

The intended outcome is a structured method for connecting observed attack behavior to candidate defenses, estimating remaining severity, and identifying promising defense combinations for further evaluation.
