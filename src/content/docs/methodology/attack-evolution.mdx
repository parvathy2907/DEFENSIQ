Changing the behavior of malware to defeat behavior-based analysis is a known attack strategy; for example, replacement attacks rewrite system-call dependences into semantically equivalent ones [30]. Here the change is limited to swapping signatures for related ones.

## Idea and Execution

Figure 3.7 shows the execution; the numbers in the figure are the step numbers below:

1. **Upload.** The user uploads one sample or the full dataset and chooses "Also evolve the attack".
2. **Iteration 1.** The defense CCEA finds the best defense combination for the original sample. Its score F<sub>0</sub> is the starting point.
3. **Evolve the attack.** The attack CCEA builds an evolved sample (see the two sections below).
4. **Public-repository check.** The evolved sample is compared with a held-out repository of real ransomware. The count is recorded and never used to steer the search.
5. **Defense.** The defense CCEA runs on the evolved sample and returns its score F<sub>g</sub>. Every choice that took part in the sample receives the credit 1 − F<sub>g</sub>.
6. **Stop rule.** After each generation, check whether every choice has been tried and 20 generations have passed without a harder sample, or 100 generations are reached. If not, go back to step 3; if yes, go to step 7.
7. **Report.** The report gives the best defense for the original sample and for the hardest evolved sample found.

## Slots, Choices and Related Signatures

A sample is a list of signatures. To evolve it, we give every signature of the sample one **slot**. A slot is a position in the sample that can be filled by a different signature. The **choices** of a slot are the original signature itself (so the slot may stay unchanged) and the signatures related to it.

Two signatures are **related** if they share at least one tag from the Malware Behavior Catalog (MBC) mapping of the Cuckoo signatures [21] or an ATT&CK technique ID [22] written in the Cuckoo signature files. This gives 31 groups of related signatures; 67 of the 91 signatures that have defense data have at least one related signature. If a signature has no related signature, its slot gets four random signatures from the pool of 91, drawn once at the start and kept for the whole run.

One evolved sample is built by taking exactly one choice from every slot (Figure 3.8). For example, in Sample01 the slot of `creates_hidden_file` can be filled by itself, `stealth_hiddenfile` or `stealth_hidden_extension`, because these signatures share the ATT&CK tags T1158 or T1564; the evolved sample takes `stealth_hidden_extension`. The slot of `console_output` has no related signature, so it receives four random choices (`has_pdb`, `creates_shortcut`, `injection_runpe` and one more), and the evolved sample takes `injection_runpe`. The slot of `ransomware_shadowcopy` keeps its own signature. A sample never contains the same signature twice, and a signature with a blank ID or without defense data stays fixed.

We use only public tags and invent no labels. Related does not mean equivalent: a shared tag says that two behaviors are linked, not that they do the same thing.

## Attack CCEA

The attack CCEA is the defense CCEA turned around. Both search with the same rules for picking a choice and for stopping, but the defense CCEA chooses defenses and is scored by F, while the attack CCEA chooses a signature for every slot and is scored by 1 − F, the share of the severity that is left after the defense CCEA has done its best. One combination takes one choice from every slot and is therefore one new sample. A choice that tends to give a harder sample (a lower F) receives a higher credit and is picked more often.

| | **Defense CCEA** (Algorithm 1) | **Attack CCEA** (Algorithm 2) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Goal** | Find the defense combination that removes the most severity (highest F). | Find the evolved sample from which the defense removes the least severity (lowest F), i.e. the sample that is hardest to defend. |
| **What stays fixed** | The signatures of the sample. | The defense CCEA: it is called unchanged for every evolved sample. |
| **Sub-populations (one per signature)** | The pool of candidate defenses of the signature. | The slot of the signature: the signature itself plus its related signatures (or four random ones). |
| **One solution** | One defense from every pool = a complete defense for the sample. | One choice from every slot = one complete evolved sample. |
| **How a solution is scored** | F(C) = (IS − RA) / IS: the share of the severity that the defenses remove (higher = better defense). | The defense CCEA is run on the evolved sample and returns its best F. The attack score is 1 − F: the share of the severity that is left (higher = harder to defend). |
| **Credit** | Every defense in the combination receives F. | Every signature choice in the evolved sample receives 1 − F. |
| **How a choice is picked (same rules in both)** | For every sub-population: (1) a choice that has not been tried yet; otherwise (2) with probability p<sub>e</sub> = 0.25 a random choice; otherwise (3) the choice with the best average credit. Then, with probability p<sub>m</sub> = 0.20, the pick is replaced by another choice of the same sub-population (only if it has more than one choice). | Same as the defense CCEA. |
| **Counts as an improvement** | F is higher than the best F so far (F > F* + δ). | F is lower than the best F so far (F < F* − δ): a harder sample was found. |
| **When it stops (same rule in both)** | Every choice has been tried at least once and 20 generations have passed without an improvement, or 100 generations are reached. | Same as the defense CCEA. |
| **Result** | C*: the best defense combination, with its score F*. | A*: the hardest evolved sample, with its best defense C*; and C<sub>0</sub>, the best defense of the original sample. |

The defense CCEA is used unchanged as a subroutine, DefenseCCEA(·): for the sample it is given, it returns the best defense combination C and its score F, and the attack CCEA uses 1 − F as its own score. A sample is harder the lower its F is. Algorithm 2 keeps the hardest evolved sample found so far, A*, together with its score F* and its best defense C*. Notation: s<sub>k</sub> is the k-th signature of the sample h (and the original signature of slot k); S<sub>k</sub> is the set of choices of slot k; a<sub>k</sub> is the choice taken in slot k; A<sub>g</sub> is the evolved sample of generation g and F<sub>g</sub> its score; n counts the generations without a harder sample. The comments name the matching step of Figure 3.7.

```text
Algorithm 2: Attack CCEA, evolving one sample h by swapping signatures for related ones
             (the defense CCEA scores every evolved sample)
Input:   sample h with signatures s_1..s_N; related-signature graph G;
         defense CCEA (Algorithm 1, unchanged);
         p_e = 0.25, p_m = 0.20, τ = 20, δ = 1e-8, G_max = 100  (as in Algorithm 1)
Output:  hardest evolved sample A*; best defense C_0 for the original sample h;
         best defense C* for A*

for each s_k:                                          # build the slots
    S_k ← {s_k} ∪ related(s_k, G)
    if s_k has no related signature:  S_k ← {s_k} ∪ 4 random signatures
(C_0, F_0) ← DefenseCCEA(h)                            # step 2: iteration 1
credit each s_k with 1 − F_0;  A* ← h;  F* ← F_0;  C* ← C_0;  n ← 0
for g = 1 .. G_max:
    for each slot k:                                   # step 3: one choice per slot
        a_k ← untried choice of S_k, else random choice of S_k if rand() < p_e,
              else best-credited choice of S_k
        if rand() < p_m and |S_k| > 1:  a_k ← another choice of S_k
        if a_k is already used in this sample:  a_k ← s_k
    A_g ← {a_1..a_N}                                   # one evolved sample
    Lookup(A_g, public repository)                     # step 4: recorded, not used
    (C_g, F_g) ← DefenseCCEA(A_g); credit each a_k with 1 − F_g     # step 5
    if F_g < F* − δ:                                   # a harder sample (lower F) was found
        A*, F*, C* ← A_g, F_g, C_g;  n ← 0
    else:
        n ← n + 1
    if every choice has been tried and n ≥ τ:  break   # step 6: stop rule
return A*, C_0, C*
```

Two simplifications are made on purpose. A signature that is swapped in has no rows in the original sample, so it borrows its defense candidates and severities from one sample of the dataset where it really appears (the donor is drawn once per signature per run); these rows are a stand-in, not a measurement. The defense CCEA is re-run from scratch on every evolved sample with the sample's own seed. In these runs the 32 rows without a `Signature_ID` are kept as fixed slots, so the iteration-1 scores differ slightly from the per-sample defense results (mean 0.789 instead of 0.790).

## Held-Out Check Against a Public Repository

We look up each evolved sample in MLRan [20], a public behavioral dataset of over 4,800 samples (ransomware and goodware); we use its Cuckoo-analysed ransomware part, 2,330 real ransomware samples from 64 families. The repository is held out: the attack never uses it to choose anything, so it is an independent check.

A whole-list match is not possible, because real samples in the repository list about 9 signatures and our samples 8 to 55. We therefore ask a weaker question: does the evolved sample contain the complete signature list of at least one real ransomware sample that uses at least one swapped-in signature? Such a real sample is inside the evolved sample only because of the evolution. This shows that the evolved sample contains behavior combinations that real ransomware has been seen to have. It does not show that the evolved malware would still run.

---

## Co-Evolution of Attack and Defense

The two CCEAs are coupled through one score: the defense's F is the attack's fitness (1 − F), and the defense is re-optimised against every evolved attack, so each side adapts to the other. The defense restarts from scratch on each evolved sample, so this is alternating co-evolution rather than one shared population. Competitive co-evolution of attack and defense has been used to find network defenses that hold up against changing attacks [27], and the dynamics of evolving attackers and defenders have been studied with evolutionary game theory [5], [28]. Our two CCEAs each follow cooperative co-evolution [17], [29] and are coupled through the score F.

The measured results are on the [Results](/evaluation/results/#attack-evolution-results) page.

---

## Limitations

- Swapped-in signatures borrow rows from another sample.
- Related signatures share a tag but are not equivalent.
- Evolved samples are signature sets and were not run in the sandbox.
- The repository check shows co-occurrence in real ransomware, not that the malware would run.
- The defense search is randomised, so part of the change in F is search noise.
