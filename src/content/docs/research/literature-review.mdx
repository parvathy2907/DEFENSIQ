Ransomware research spans dynamic malware analysis, early threat detection, behavioral feature selection, attack–defense mapping, severity quantification, and automated defense optimization. These research areas provide the foundations for the proposed framework, which combines behavioral evidence from Cuckoo Sandbox with MITRE ATT&CK–D3FEND mapping, LLM-based residual-severity estimation, and Cooperative Co-Evolutionary Algorithm (CCEA)-based defense selection.

This review compares representative studies according to their methodology, primary research focus, and relevance to the present work. The objective is to identify how existing approaches contribute to ransomware analysis and defense, and to determine which parts of the attack–defense decision-making process remain to be addressed by the proposed framework.

## Key Observations and Synthesis

### 1. Dynamic Malware Analysis Provides Behavioral Evidence
Dynamic malware analysis allows researchers to observe program execution and collect evidence such as API-call sequences, process activity, and file-system modifications [2]. Studies such as Al-Rimy et al. [3] and Ramesh and Menen [4] demonstrate how behavioral information can support ransomware detection.

In the proposed framework, Cuckoo Sandbox provides the execution reports from which triggered behavioral signatures are extracted. These signatures serve as structured inputs for subsequent attack characterization and severity assessment. However, the behaviors recorded in a sandbox represent the activity observed during a particular execution, not necessarily every capability of the analyzed sample.

### 2. Detection and Defense Optimization Address Different Problems
Several reviewed studies focus on detecting ransomware, identifying relevant behavioral features, or forecasting malware trends [3], [4], [6], [7], [9]–[12]. These functions are important, but a successful detection result does not automatically determine which defensive mechanisms are appropriate or how much risk remains after applying them.

The present project addresses the subsequent decision-making problem: given an observed attack behavior and a candidate defense, estimate the residual severity and use that assessment to compare possible defense combinations.

### 3. ATT&CK and D3FEND Provide Complementary Representations
MITRE ATT&CK provides a structured representation of adversarial tactics and techniques [13]. MITRE D3FEND provides a structured vocabulary of defensive techniques, artifacts, and relationships [14].

These resources help organize attack and defense information into a common analytical framework. However, a mapping relationship alone does not establish that a defense completely mitigates an attack. D3FEND explicitly distinguishes the description of countermeasure capabilities from the assessment and prioritization of their effectiveness. Consequently, the proposed framework requires a separate evaluation stage that considers the observed attack behavior and the defensive mechanism when estimating residual severity.

### 4. Impact Quantification Supports Attack-Severity Assessment
The MAGIC approach scores signatures in two phases: it first places each signature in a severity band using its severity and frequency, then refines the score using mutual information among signatures that occur together [15]. This is relevant because individual triggered signatures may represent different aspects of a malware sample's behavior, and their significance may depend on the behaviors observed together.

The project incorporates signature impact quantification to provide severity-related inputs to the attack–defense evaluation process. The subsequent LLM-based assessment addresses a different question: how much severity may remain when a particular defensive mechanism is considered.

### 5. LLM-Based Assessment Requires Task-Specific Evaluation
Large Language Models have been investigated for a range of cybersecurity applications, including security analysis and threat-intelligence-related tasks [18]. Their ability to interpret structured behavioral evidence makes them candidates for reasoning about attack–defense relationships.

However, general cybersecurity reasoning capability does not establish that a model will estimate residual severity accurately. The model must receive appropriate evidence, produce valid structured outputs, and be evaluated against reference labels. For this reason, the project treats LLM-based residual-severity estimation as an empirical component whose errors and limitations must be measured.

### 6. Evolutionary Optimization Addresses the Combinatorial Search Challenge
Cooperative co-evolutionary algorithms decompose a complex optimization problem into cooperating subproblems and evolve candidate solutions through separate populations [17]. This provides a foundation for searching problems in which the quality of a complete solution depends on multiple interacting components.

In this project, the candidate defense combination is evaluated using residual-severity estimates and the defined fitness function. CCEA searches the candidate space and retains promising combinations rather than explicitly enumerating every possible configuration. The quality of the selected defense strategy consequently depends on the candidate defense pool, the RS estimates, the fitness formulation, and the search configuration.

## Research Gap and Positioning of the Proposed Work

The reviewed studies establish important foundations in ransomware behavior analysis, early detection, feature selection, severity quantification, standardized threat mapping, LLM-assisted cybersecurity, and evolutionary optimization. Within this reviewed set, these capabilities are addressed primarily as separate research tasks.

The problem motivating this project is the need to connect these capabilities in a single decision-support workflow. Specifically, the framework combines:

1. **Cuckoo Sandbox-based dynamic malware analysis** and behavioral-signature extraction.
2. **ATT&CK-based characterization** of observed adversarial behavior.
3. **ATT&CK–D3FEND mapping** to identify candidate defensive mechanisms.
4. **Signature impact quantification** to establish severity-related information.
5. **LLM-based residual-severity estimation** for individual attack–defense pairs.
6. **CCEA-based evaluation and selection** of candidate defense combinations.

The central research question is whether residual-severity estimates can provide useful evaluation signals for an evolutionary search over candidate defenses, and whether the resulting defense combinations reduce the Malware Severity Score from MSS<sub>before</sub> = IS to MSS<sub>after</sub> = RA(C*), as defined on the [CCEA page](/methodology/ccea/), and whether they outperform a greedy baseline under the project's experimental conditions.

The framework therefore differs from detection-only approaches by focusing on the relationship between observed attack behavior, defensive mechanisms, remaining severity, and defense-combination selection. Its effectiveness must be established through the evaluation of RS predictions and the comparison of optimized defense combinations against appropriate baselines.
