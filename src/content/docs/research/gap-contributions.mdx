This section identifies the research challenges that motivate the proposed ransomware defense optimization framework. It explains how the project connects dynamic malware analysis, standardized attack–defense mapping, signature impact quantification, LLM-based residual-severity estimation, and evolutionary optimization into a single workflow.

---

## From Phase 1 to Phase 2: How the Gap Moved

The Phase 1 review (Panel Review 2, 30 March 2026) identified gaps in ransomware **detection**:

- The need to handle dynamic adaptation in early-detection models to combat evolving ransomware.
- The need to refine existing models to minimize false positives while still identifying ransomware accurately.
- The need for an effective method to identify stealthy variants that evade traditional detection.
- The need for comprehensive early-detection approaches that consider a wide range of indicators and behaviors.

### Challenges noted in Phase 1

- Rapidly evolving ransomware variants [8].
- Evasive malware techniques [11].
- Fileless and stealthy attacks.
- The complexity of early detection [6].
- The lack of adaptive defense mechanisms.

### Why Phase 2 focuses on defense selection

The last challenge, the lack of adaptive defense mechanisms, is the one the reviewed literature leaves most open. Every study in the [literature review](/research/literature-review/) ends once ransomware has been detected or forecast; none of them recommends which defenses to apply afterwards. Phase 2 therefore treats detection as an input and focuses on the next decision: given an observed behavior, which defenses should be applied, and how much severity will remain? The four gaps below are the research questions that follow from that shift.

## Research Gaps

Existing research has contributed methods for ransomware detection, behavioral analysis, severity quantification, threat characterization, and defense optimization. However, integrating these capabilities into a workflow that evaluates individual attack–defense pairs and selects effective defense combinations remains the central challenge addressed by this project.

### Gap 1: Disconnect Between Threat Modeling and Defense Evaluation

MITRE ATT&CK provides a structured representation of adversarial tactics and techniques, while MITRE D3FEND organizes defensive techniques and their relationships to digital artifacts [13], [14]. These resources support standardized attack–defense mapping, but identifying a candidate defense does not by itself establish how effectively it mitigates a particular observed behavior.

The challenge addressed in this project is to connect sandbox-generated behavioral signatures and ATT&CK techniques with candidate D3FEND defenses, and then assess the likely effect of each defense on the corresponding attack behavior.

### Gap 2: Connecting Signature Impact Quantification With Residual Severity

Existing research has investigated methods for quantifying malware impact. The MAGIC approach, for example, uses signature co-occurrence and mutual-information-based refinement to improve the representation of behavioral impact [15]. However, an estimate of a signature's impact and an estimate of the severity remaining after applying a defense address different questions.

The challenge is to use severity-related information from observed malware behavior in conjunction with an assessment of defensive effectiveness. The initial impact score alone does not establish whether a particular defense prevents, restricts, detects, or leaves the attack behavior possible.

### Gap 3: Efficient Selection of Defense Combinations

Evolutionary algorithms and cooperative co-evolutionary methods provide ways to search complex optimization spaces without explicitly enumerating every possible solution [5], [17]. In ransomware defense, each observed signature may have multiple candidate defensive mechanisms, and the quality of a complete defense combination depends on the estimated outcomes of its constituent attack–defense pairs.

The challenge is to evaluate candidate combinations efficiently when the number of possible configurations becomes large. A defense that is useful for one behavior may not be the most suitable choice for another, making systematic combination-level evaluation necessary.

    The framework uses CCEA to generate candidate defense combinations, evaluate them using the project's fitness function, and retain promising solutions across successive generations. This provides an evolutionary search strategy for defense selection. ### Gap 4: Reliability of LLM-Based Residual-Severity Estimation

LLMs can interpret structured cybersecurity evidence and generate assessments of attack–defense relationships. However, a plausible explanation does not necessarily establish that a predicted residual-severity value is correct. Inconsistent outputs, insufficient evidence, or incorrect assumptions about defensive capabilities may affect the quality of an assessment.

For the proposed framework, the important research challenge is therefore to measure whether LLM-generated residual-severity estimates are sufficiently consistent and accurate for use as inputs to an optimization algorithm.

## Motivation for the Proposed Approach

The motivation for this framework is to provide a systematic connection between observed ransomware behavior and the selection of defensive mechanisms. The project brings together components that address different stages of this decision-making process.

First, Cuckoo Sandbox supplies behavioral reports and triggered signatures from controlled malware execution. These reports are parsed to extract the evidence required to characterize observed attack behavior. Second, MITRE ATT&CK is used to organize the identified behaviors into standardized tactics, techniques, and sub-techniques. The project's ATT&CK–D3FEND mapping process then identifies candidate defensive mechanisms relevant to those techniques.

Third, signature impact quantification provides severity-related scores, incorporating signature co-occurrence and mutual-information-based refinement. An LLM evaluates individual attack–defense pairs and estimates the residual severity that may remain after considering a candidate defense. Finally, CCEA uses these estimates to evaluate and compare defense combinations through the project's fitness function.

This integrated approach is motivated by three considerations:

1. **Structured evidence:** Decisions are based on observed behavioral information and standardized attack–defense mappings rather than relying solely on signature names or unstructured descriptions.
2. **Context-sensitive assessment:** The residual-severity stage considers the relationship between an observed attack behavior and a candidate defense instead of assigning a fixed severity reduction to every defense category.
3. **Scalable search:** CCEA searches the defense-combination space iteratively, providing a practical alternative to exhaustive enumeration when many signatures and candidate defenses are involved.

The overall motivation is to make defense selection more systematic, measurable, and adaptable to the attack scenarios represented in the dataset. The effectiveness of the approach must be established through empirical evaluation of both residual-severity predictions and the defense combinations selected by the optimization process.

## Key Research Contributions

The project contributes an integrated framework for ransomware defense analysis and optimization. Its principal contributions are as follows:

### 1. Integration of Dynamic Malware Analysis With Behavioral Signature Processing
The framework processes Cuckoo Sandbox reports to identify triggered signatures and relevant execution evidence. This establishes the behavioral input used by subsequent attack characterization and defense assessment.

### 2. Standardized Attack–Defense Representation
The framework connects observed behavioral signatures with MITRE ATT&CK techniques and identifies candidate defensive mechanisms through ATT&CK–D3FEND mappings. This provides a structured representation of the relationship between observed attacks and available defenses.

### 3. Integration of Signature Impact Quantification and Residual Severity
The framework incorporates the project's signature impact quantification approach and uses severity-related information in conjunction with LLM-based residual-severity estimation. This distinguishes the estimated impact of an observed behavior from the estimated severity remaining when a defensive mechanism is considered.

### 4. LLM-Based Attack–Defense Assessment
The framework uses an LLM to evaluate individual attack–defense pairs using the supplied behavioral and defense information. The resulting residual-severity estimates are used as quantitative inputs to the defense-combination optimization process. The quality of these estimates is assessed using reference labels and defined evaluation metrics.

### 5. CCEA-Based Defense-Combination Optimization
The framework formulates defense selection as a search over candidate combinations. CCEA generates and evaluates combinations using the project's severity-based fitness function and retains promising candidates across generations. This avoids the need to enumerate the full combination space explicitly.

### 6. Experimental Evaluation of Severity Estimation and Optimization
The residual-severity component is evaluated with QWK, normalized MAE, exact-match accuracy, tolerance accuracy and macro-averaged F1 for the mitigation labels. The defense-optimization component is evaluated with the Malware Severity Score before and after the recommended defenses, a comparison against a greedy baseline using the same pools and RS source, and paired statistical testing (Shapiro–Wilk, then the Wilcoxon signed-rank test at α = 0.05). A BeagleBoyz/APT38–HOPLIGHT case study walks through the integrated workflow. See [Evaluation Methodology](/evaluation/evaluation-plan/).

<p style="font-size: 0.85rem; color: var(--sl-color-gray-3); margin-top: 0.75rem;">
  These contributions are intended to be evaluated in terms of the framework's implementation, prediction quality, optimization behavior, and performance on the studied dataset. Claims of superiority over competing systems require direct comparative experiments.
</p>
